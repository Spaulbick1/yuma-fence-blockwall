A retaining wall holds back a grade change — a raised planter bed, a sloped side yard, a cut for a driveway or patio — and unlike a perimeter or decorative wall, it carries real structural load from the soil and water pressure behind it. That load is exactly why Arizona licenses this work separately from ordinary fencing.

## Why this is R-31 work, not R-14

Arizona ROC's classification list draws a clear, explicit line here: **R-14 (Fencing)** covers metal, wood, and cement block fencing, automatic gates, and cattle guards — and specifically **excludes retaining-wall installation**. **R-31 (Masonry)** is the broader classification that covers structural masonry, concrete block, and related work, and is what a genuine retaining wall falls under.

In practice, this means a contractor who only holds an R-14 license isn't licensed to build a structural retaining wall, even if they're an experienced, reputable fence installer. This is a genuinely useful thing to check before hiring — not a technicality, since the license distinction tracks a real difference in the engineering knowledge a retaining wall actually requires (soil load, drainage design, footing depth) versus what a fence needs. See the [full R-14 vs. R-31 license guide](/guides/fence-masonry-license-arizona/) for the complete breakdown, and verify any specific contractor's license classification directly at [roc.az.gov](https://roc.az.gov) before signing a contract.

## What drives cost and risk

- **Height.** Cost and engineering requirements both increase disproportionately as wall height increases — a low, 18-inch garden wall is a very different project from a 4-foot retaining wall holding back a driveway cut.
- **Drainage design.** Weep holes, gravel backfill, and sometimes a drain pipe behind the wall are what prevent hydrostatic pressure from building up and pushing the wall outward over time. This is the single most common point of failure on a poorly-built retaining wall, and it's also the easiest thing for a low bid to skip since it's invisible once the wall is finished.
- **Footing depth and reinforcement.** Needs to be sized to the specific soil and load — not a generic number reused across every job.
- **Soil conditions.** Much of the Yuma area sits on caliche, a naturally cemented calcium-carbonate hardpan common across the Sonoran Desert, which can affect both excavation cost and how a footing needs to be designed.
- **Permit and engineering review.** Taller walls, or walls near a property line, canal, or drainage easement, may require a permit and engineered plans — confirm current City of Yuma requirements before finalizing scope.

## Vetting checklist

- Confirm the contractor holds an active **R-31 (Masonry)** license — verify it yourself at roc.az.gov, since this scope specifically requires it.
- Ask directly how the design handles drainage behind the wall — a vague or dismissive answer here is a real warning sign, not a minor detail.
- Get footing depth and reinforcement specs in writing, sized to your wall's actual height and soil.
- For any wall above a few feet, ask whether the project needs a permit or engineering sign-off under current City of Yuma rules, and confirm who's responsible for pulling it.

## Red flags

- A bid that doesn't mention drainage at all.
- A license that verifies as R-14 (Fencing) rather than R-31 (Masonry) for a genuine load-bearing retaining wall.
- A flat per-foot price quoted without a site visit — height and soil conditions change the engineering enough that a responsible contractor needs to see the actual grade.
- Reluctance to discuss permit requirements for a wall that's clearly tall enough to need one.
