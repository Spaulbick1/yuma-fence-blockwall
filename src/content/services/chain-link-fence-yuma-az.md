Chain link is the most common budget-friendly fencing option in Yuma — durable, low-maintenance, and typically the fastest to install of the standard fence materials, which is part of why it's the least expensive per linear foot.

## What's involved

A standard chain link installation includes setting terminal posts (corners, ends, and gate posts) in concrete, setting line posts at regular intervals (typically 8–10 feet apart), stretching the mesh fabric between posts under tension, and attaching top rail and tension wire. Gauge (wire thickness) and coating (galvanized vs. vinyl-coated) are the two biggest quality variables — a heavier gauge and a quality coating both cost more up front but hold up longer against Yuma's sun and occasional monsoon wind.

As with any fence type set into Yuma-area soil, caliche — a naturally cemented, calcium-carbonate hardpan common across the Sonoran Desert — can slow post-hole digging in parts of the property. It's a smaller cost factor for chain link than for a heavier privacy fence (fewer, smaller-diameter post holes), but it's still worth asking about if your lot has known hardpan.

## Cost factors

- **Gauge and coating.** Standard galvanized chain link is the base-cost option; vinyl-coated (typically black or green) costs more but resists fading and looks more finished for a front-facing yard.
- **Height.** 4-foot is standard for a residential perimeter or pet enclosure; taller heights (5–6 feet) for security or livestock cost more per foot.
- **Gate count and width.** Single walk gates are inexpensive to add; double-drive gates for vehicle or RV access cost meaningfully more.
- **Top rail vs. no top rail.** A top rail adds rigidity and a more finished look but adds material cost.
- **Terrain and soil.** Caliche hardpan or a sloped lot can add labor time for post setting.

## Where chain link fits in Yuma

Chain link is a common choice for: side and back property-line fencing where privacy isn't the priority, pet containment areas, RV/trailer pad enclosures (common on Yuma's larger residential and semi-rural lots), garden or agricultural-adjacent fencing, and temporary or budget-conscious perimeter fencing ahead of a later upgrade. It's less commonly used as a primary privacy fence or a pool barrier on its own, since it offers minimal visual screening and is climbable without an added barrier feature.

## Vetting checklist

- Confirm the Arizona ROC license classification — chain link installation falls under **R-14 (Fencing)**. See the [R-14 vs. R-31 license guide](/guides/fence-masonry-license-arizona/) if your project also involves any masonry work.
- Verify any contractor's license status directly at [roc.az.gov](https://roc.az.gov) before signing — we work with providers who report being licensed and insured, but confirm it yourself.
- Ask specifically about wire gauge and coating type in the written quote — "chain link" alone isn't a complete spec, and gauge is the single biggest driver of how long the fence lasts.
- Get post spacing and depth in writing, particularly for taller (5–6 ft) installations or wind-exposed lots.
- If the fence is anywhere near a pool, confirm in writing whether it's being represented as meeting current Yuma pool-barrier code — don't assume a standard chain-link installation qualifies without confirmation.

## Red flags

- A per-foot price with no gauge or coating spec attached — that's often a sign of a lower-grade material being quoted at a competitive-looking price.
- No mention of terminal-post concrete depth, especially on corners and gate posts, which carry the most structural load.
- A license number that doesn't verify as active at roc.az.gov.
